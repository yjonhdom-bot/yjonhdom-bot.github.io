Jacquard webbing and brand tape

  • Product Introduction

Posted by TrimGem On Aug 01 2024

Jacquard webbing is a narrow fabric whose pattern is woven in: brand names, logos, geometrics, stripes that are yarn, not ink. It is not a print. It is not embroidery. It is a loom choosing which coloured warps sit on the face. That is why a jacquard strap still looks like itself after a season of abrasion, and why a 4 mm Chinese-character logo on a 10 mm tape will not.

TrimGem samples jacquard tapes next to woven labels and printed tapes so a brand can pick the construction that matches the logo, not the construction that was in the last shipment.

Jacquard tape versus a woven label

A woven label (main label, size label) usually holds the warp and paints the motif with weft. A jacquard webbing usually holds the weft and paints the motif with warp, on a small needle loom. Pattern changes mean re-threading and loom time. That is why jacquard tape MOQs and strike-offs are a scheduling conversation, not a “send the PNG.”

Both can carry a logo. They are different articles:

  • Webbing is a strap or binding you sew as a tape: bag handles, belt loops, hoodie channels, shoe overlays, lanyards, pet collars.
  • A woven label is a cut piece you sew or heat-seal as identification.

Shoe-tongue and heel jacquard patches (often 20×30 mm or 25×35 mm, high-density polyester, heat-cut or folded, a few tenths of a gram) sit between the two: a small woven badge, sewn like a label, built like a tape.

Printed tape (ink or transfer on a finished webbing) is cheaper for photos, gradients and high colour counts. It also crocks, cracks and peels. If the logo must survive a backpack strap, weave it.

Fibre, width and thickness

Polyester is the volume jacquard: colour range, heat-cut edges, price. Nylon is used when the strap needs nylon’s hand, abrasion or dye story. Cotton and blends appear on heritage and kids’ programs.

Width is the canvas. Narrow tapes decorate a garment or a shoe. Mid widths become handles and lanyards. Wide tapes exist so a logo can be read. A complex mark that does not fit the width will be redesigned by the loom — usually badly.

Thickness matters when the tape must pass a buckle or a slider. A plush jacquard that cannot feed a ladder lock is a hardware problem invented by the textile.

Edges: heat-cut, woven selvedge, or folded-and-sewn. Heat-cut polyester should be smooth, without beads of melt that scratch skin or catch a sewing foot.

What a loom can and cannot do

  • Logos, Latin type, simple Chinese, geometrics: yes, if the width and the yarn count allow the stroke.
  • Dense Chinese at small point sizes: often no. Review the artwork on the actual width before you lock a moulded buckle to that tape.
  • Photographs, fine illustration, continuous gradients: not a jacquard job. Print, or simplify.
  • Colour count: every extra warp colour is a beam and a cost. Lock brand colours first.
  • Face and back: they are not automatically the same. A reverse-colour back is common. Ask for a two-face design if the tape will flip.

Artwork should arrive as vectors (AI / PDF). Bitmaps get interpreted by a designer who has never seen the garment. Shade lots on polyester jacquard still need a crock, a wash and a light-fastness number if the tape sits in the sun.

Fastness and handling

A decent jacquard should keep the mark through rub, wash and sweat: no bleed, no “peeling” (there is nothing to peel if it was woven), no exploded edge after a fold. Yarn density is the anti-fray system. Store cool, dry, out of sun, away from sharp metal, not crushed in a stack that flats the floats.

Spec line

Write fibre, width, thickness, weave/jacquard, colour count, face/back, edge, and whether this is metre tape or a cut badge. Attach the vector and a stated minimum stroke. If the mark is a care-label paragraph, use a woven label. If it is a brand marching down a strap, use jacquard webbing. If it is a photograph of the founder, use a printer — and do not call that webbing.
